A guide to AI protein design

We can choose where a protein should bind

Antibody drugs already work. So why design a new protein from scratch? Because sometimes the job calls for a different shape, a smaller molecule, or a binding site that existing antibodies miss. We can ask for those properties before making the first batch.

From a sequence on screen to activity in the lab

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a shape. A binder is a protein that attaches to another molecule. A prediction model estimates the shape of a sequence. With generative design, we can work the other way: start with the contact we want and propose new sequences that might make it. Using a prediction model to search for such sequences is sometimes called protein hallucination.

  1. Choose the job

    Pick the target and, where possible, the patch to bind. Decide what the protein should do and which related targets it should avoid.

  2. Generate designs

    Propose sequences and rank them by how well their predicted shapes fit the job. Change the design brief and run another round without remaking the target.

  3. Measure what works

    Make a library, run biological selection, then express and purify individual hits. Measure binding, selectivity and activity. Feed the results into the next round.

Three ways to find a binder

Hybridomas and display libraries are proven ways to discover antibodies. AI changes how candidates enter the screen. It can also design antibody fragments, so these approaches can work together.

MethodWhere candidates come fromWhat you can steer early
HybridomaImmunise an animal, fuse its antibody-producing cells with cells that keep growing, then screen the resulting clones.Choose what to immunise with and which clones to keep. The immune response supplies the antibodies.
Phage or yeast displayScreen a physical library of proteins displayed on phages or yeast. Keep variants that bind and repeat.Set the selection conditions. You can favour a particular site or remove binders that recognise related proteins.
AI design + biological selectionGenerate a library for the brief from a target model or, in some cases, a sequence. Make selected designs and test them.Specify the intended binding site and design for size or geometry before ordering DNA. The assays determine what worked.

Design can begin while assays are being set up, without waiting for immunisation. In one RFdiffusion study, researchers found binders for five targets after testing fewer than 100 designs per target. To compare methods on a new target, ask how long it takes to get a binder that passes the same tests for selectivity and activity.

When the target is hard to purify. Starting from a model or sequence means teams can launch design without a vial of purified target. Some campaigns can also screen against cells displaying the target. Classic display can do that too. The right assay depends on what the binder needs to recognise.

What can you ask a protein to do?

Bind one target and leave a close relative alone

A team designed binders for short signalling peptides from their sequences. One binder recognised glucagon and showed selectivity over secretin. That is the kind of difference you can design for and measure directly.

Hit a specific surface

Researchers designed an antibody fragment for a chosen site on the C. difficile toxin TcdB. It neutralised the toxin in a cell assay and did not bind the related toxin TcsL.

Bring two proteins together

Binding can be a means to an end. For example, we can design a binder to contact two proteins at once and test whether bringing them together changes what they do. Which surfaces meet and at what angle become part of the design brief.
