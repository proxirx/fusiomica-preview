Phase 0: measure where the binder goes

We plan to give a tiny amount of radiolabelled binder in a supervised study. PET shows how much tracer reaches tumours, where else it goes and how long it stays. A microdose keeps drug exposure low and can reduce the nonclinical work needed for an initial study. The scan gives us an early delivery readout. Later studies assess treatment benefit.

FDA guidance on microdose radiopharmaceuticals

Faster design needs earlier human data

Eroom’s law describes a long decline in drug R&D productivity. Historically, approvals per billion dollars of research spending, adjusted for inflation, roughly halved every nine years. We want to shorten the time it takes to get an answer in people.

AI helps us build binders for targets that are hard to pursue. A supervised Phase 0 PET study gives us evidence in people as soon as the safety work and approvals allow. We use the uptake data to decide whether to advance, redesign or stop.

Scannell et al. on Eroom’s law · What will it take for AI to change drug discovery?

Too many programmes chase the same targets

A less explored target can look too risky to justify a large trial. A small PET study can first show whether the binder reaches human tumours and where else it accumulates. That narrows the delivery risk and gives us evidence to decide whether a larger investment is warranted.

Bruce Booth on pipeline crowding · LifeSciVC
How ADCs and radioligands deliver treatment

A radionuclide decays at a known rate. By measuring uptake and retention, we can estimate the radiation dose to tumours and healthy organs. Treatment response is a separate clinical question.
