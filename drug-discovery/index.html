One cancer marker, two ways to use it

We’re developing one binder for a marker found across cancer types. It could help us locate tumours and deliver treatment to the same target.

What is theranostics?

Theranostics pairs imaging and treatment around the same target. Our approach starts with one protein binder: attach an imaging label to see where it goes, or a therapeutic radionuclide to deliver radiation to those sites.

Lead optimisation and imaging in cancer cells

Phase 0: measure where the binder goes

  1. Give a microdose
  2. Scan with PET
  3. Map uptake

A first-in-human PET study uses a microdose of the radiolabelled binder. The endpoint is imaging: how much tracer reaches tumour lesions, where else it goes and how long it stays. Eligible microdose tracers can enter the clinic with a narrower scope of manufacturing and controls (CMC) and a smaller nonclinical safety package. Clinical-grade production, quality controls and dosimetry remain part of the work.

FDA guidance on microdose radiopharmaceuticals

Test less crowded targets in people

  1. Choose a target
  2. Measure uptake in people
  3. Advance or redesign

We can design binders for underexplored targets with promising biology. Animal studies help assess safety and dosimetry. Phase 0 PET asks the question those models can only approximate: does the binder reach human tumours? The answer helps us decide what to advance, redesign or stop before committing to a full therapeutic programme.

Radiation gives us a measurable dose

  1. Known decay rate
  2. Measure tissue retention
  3. Estimate absorbed dose

A therapeutic radionuclide decays at a known rate and emits radiation at known energies. Measure how much reaches a tissue and how long it stays, and we can estimate the dose. That is a more direct readout than for many antibody–drug conjugates, where cellular uptake, linker cleavage and drug release also matter. Tumour response still depends on both dose and tumour biology.

EANM guidance on dosimetry · FDA guidance on ADCs

Drug discovery learns too slowly

Eroom’s law tracks a steady fall in drug R&D productivity: the number of new drugs approved per inflation-adjusted billion dollars of R&D roughly halved every nine years. Testing in people earlier can shorten the gap between choosing a target and deciding what to do next.

Scannell et al. on Eroom’s law

Too many programmes chase the same targets

When hundreds of programmes pursue the same mechanism, it can take years to see which offer a real advantage. Earlier human data can help teams decide whether to pursue a less explored target.

Bruce Booth on pipeline crowding · LifeSciVC
